\section{Introduction}

Your body maintains a temperature of 37$^\circ$C. It doesn't drift. It doesn't fluctuate randomly. If you walk into a sauna, you sweat. If you jump into a lake, you shiver. You represent a \textbf{homeostatic system}: a system that actively expends energy to maintain a stable internal state against environmental perturbations.

Neural networks are supposed to be different. We are told they are passive function approximators, sliding down loss landscapes like marbles in a bowl. But what if they aren't?

We hypothesize that neural networks exhibit homeostatic regulation: actively maintaining information-geometric equilibria and defending them against perturbation. This paper presents early evidence for this hypothesis.

\subsection{The Hypothesis and Its Predictions}

If homeostasis is fundamental to learning systems, we should observe specific signatures:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spontaneous convergence:} Systems should find precise equilibria without explicit design.
    \item \textbf{Active compensation:} Perturbations should trigger compensatory responses from other components.
    \item \textbf{Conserved architecture:} Similar regulatory circuits should appear across scales and architectures.
\end{enumerate}

We test these predictions in transformers on algorithmic tasks, a regime where we have maximal experimental control. We then ask whether the same circuit signatures appear at production scale.

\subsection{The Critical Question: Instrumental or Fundamental?}

A deeper question underlies this work: Is homeostatic regulation \emph{instrumental} (a side effect of task-solving) or \emph{fundamental} (an intrinsic property of learning)?

If instrumental, networks should regulate information geometry only when it helps performance. If fundamental, networks should regulate geometry even when it does not help, or when performance is impossible.

We test this directly. In addition to algorithmic tasks (where networks achieve high accuracy), we train on a distributional language task where networks cannot exceed chance performance. If homeostasis is fundamental, EDI should crystallize regardless of accuracy.

The result is unambiguous: networks converge to EDI = 0.9091 with 48\% accuracy (chance level). Homeostasis is not a side effect of optimization. It is prior to it.



\subsection{Why Toy Models (And Why That's Not a Weakness)}

A valid objection: ``You're testing five random seeds on mod-2 parity. Why should anyone care?''

We care because the alternative (testing at scale) is impossible with current resources. We cannot freeze GPT-2 attention heads during training. We cannot run the kill test at Mistral scale. So we make a deliberate choice: \textit{sacrifice scale to gain mechanism}.

In toy models, we can run interventional experiments (freeze heads, perturb components, measure direct effects). At scale, we can identify circuits and measure effects, but we cannot intervene causally during training.

Our strategy: Use toy models to prove the mechanism works in a controlled regime. Use production models to ask whether similar circuits appear at scale. Neither alone proves universality. Together, they constitute early evidence worth taking seriously.

This is not weakness. It is strategy. And it is why the evidence we present (kill tests, precise measurements, cross-architecture validation) carries weight despite the small-model setting.

\subsection{What This Paper Claims (And What It Does Not)}

We claim:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Homeostatic dynamics appear spontaneously in transformers on algorithmic tasks (empirically demonstrated)
    \item Compensation is active, not passive; blocking it degrades learning (causally demonstrated)
    \item Homeostasis is independent of task performance: networks crystallize even at chance accuracy (empirically demonstrated)
    \item Similar circuit anatomy exists at production scale (observationally demonstrated)
\end{itemize}

We do \textbf{not} claim:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Homeostasis is necessary for all learning
    \item The mechanisms at scale are identical to toy models
    \item We have proven universality
\end{itemize}

The ``thermodynamics'' framing is offered as productive analogy: one that makes testable predictions and has guided our experiments. It is not a claim of literal equivalence with physical thermodynamics.

We define the \textbf{crystallization window} as the training interval between first detection of EDI standard deviation $<0.001$ (onset of inter-head agreement) and EDI std $<0.0001$ (crystallization complete). This window captures the phase transition between disordered pre-grokking dynamics and crystalline equilibrium.
